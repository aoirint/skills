# Writing Guide Rationale

These English technical and web-writing guides inform the structure review.
Their numerical suggestions are contextual editorial heuristics, not universal
limits or a basis for Japanese character caps. Apply semantic unity, audience,
genre, navigation, and preservation of meaning before counts.

## Shared background and findings

[Google's audience guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)
asks writers to account for readers' existing knowledge and how closely they
work with the subject. Use this to judge what can be treated as background
without assuming that readers know what the investigation uncovered.

[Nature Education's audience and purpose guide](https://www.nature.com/scitable/topicpage/audience-and-purpose-13952663/)
distinguishes immediate recipients from people who will read the document
later without its original context. Consider those readers when the venue and
purpose make them likely; do not add an introduction to every public reply.

The [University of Manchester's reporting examples](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/reporting-results/)
illustrate how to connect an analysis to its result. Use this when the source
of a finding helps the reader follow it. For example, use “The staging worker
uses the shared queue” as established context, and “The staging configuration
shows that failed jobs also return to the shared queue” when that is what the
inspection uncovered. Do not repeat the inspection in every sentence. These
examples are not rules for verb endings in another language.

## Link placement in discussion posts

[Massachusetts's social media accessibility guide](https://accessibility.mass.gov/training/social#hyperlinks)
puts separate links at the end of a social post: readers can skip a long link
without also skipping any text that follows. The
[University of Dundee's web guide](https://www.dundee.ac.uk/guides/writing-link-text-websites#position-links-where-it-helps-the-reader)
suggests placing a link at the end of a sentence or below the paragraph it
relates to. These are examples from different formats, not rules for GitHub
issues. Use them to place links at a natural stopping point.

[Flarum's community guidelines](https://discuss.flarum.org/d/859-flarum-community-guidelines)
ask contributors to avoid several posts in a row when one will do.
[GitHub's pull request review guide](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/how-tos/review-pull-requests/commenting-on-a-pull-request)
explains that batching inline review feedback avoids multiple notifications.
That is specific to pull request reviews. In a discussion, group work that
belongs together; keep separate replies when the venue or conversation calls for
them. Use `github-workflow` for GitHub-specific editing and posting conventions.

## Sections

[Microsoft's heading guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/headings)
recommends dividing substantial content into meaningful subtopics without
manufacturing subdivisions or filler. It sets no paragraph-count ceiling.

## Paragraphs

[Google's paragraph guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/paragraphs)
centers a paragraph on a coherent unit of reasoning. Its sentence-count advice
also warns against excessive one-sentence paragraphs. Use counts to prompt
review of focus and reader effort rather than automatic splitting.

## Sentences

[Google's short-sentence guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/short-sentences)
recommends one idea, removing unnecessary words, and clarifying complicated
embedded enumerations. It does not impose a universal word-count ceiling.
Related conditions and consequences still need an intelligible connection.

## Lists

[Microsoft's list guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists)
favors related, parallel, scannable items. Its suggested item count is qualified
by "if possible," and it permits occasional longer items.
[Google's list guidance](https://developers.google.com/style/lists) also allows
multi-paragraph items. Neither supports an unconditional one-sentence rule.

One independently reviewable point per item is this Skill's working default,
not a quoted universal rule. Closely related elements and necessary supporting
details can stay together. List length warrants regrouping only when meaningful
categories or easier scanning justify it; preserve complete requirements and order.
